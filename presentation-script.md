
View 1 — Opening and Research Question (0:45) 

Good morning everyone. Today, our group critically evaluates Punggol Digital District, or PDD, as an example of smart and ecological urbanism in Singapore. 

PDD integrates digital technologies, environmental infrastructure, education and economic development. But technological integration does not automatically establish sustainability. 

Our central question is: To what extent does PDD translate its smart and ecological ambitions into meaningful environmental, social and governance outcomes? 

We examine this through ecological sustainability, Smart City 2.0, and governance and justice, using the social-ecological-technological systems, or SETS, framework. 

Our argument is that PDD demonstrates substantial technological integration, but its wider sustainability contribution remains conditional on verified outcomes and accountable governance. 

View 2 — PDD's Vision and Systems Integration (1:00) 

PDD is a 50-hectare mixed-use district developed by JTC Corporation, bringing together the Singapore Institute of Technology, digital-economy industries and community amenities. 

With approximately 28,000 planned jobs and 12,000 students, it supports Singapore's decentralisation strategy of bringing employment and education closer to homes. 

Its infrastructure includes interconnected district cooling plants, renewable energy, a smart grid and pneumatic waste collection. These systems are supported by the Open Digital Platform, which integrates district-management information. 

The interesting question is how these systems interact. 

For example, centralised cooling can free rooftop space for solar panels and greenery, demonstrating how one infrastructure decision influences energy management and land use. 

However, systems thinking also highlights that improving individual components does not necessarily improve the sustainability of the entire district.

View 4 — Ecological Sustainability: Verification versus Ambition (1:45)  

Our first analytical perspective concerns ecological sustainability.  

PDD's interconnected district cooling plants have a reported combined capacity exceeding 30,000 refrigeration tons.  

JTC also reports that three Super Low Energy-certified buildings target at least 40% energy savings, with further savings potentially achievable through AI.  

These figures demonstrate the scale of PDD's ambitions, but they represent different measures: infrastructure capacity, design targets and projected improvements.  

[Point to the evidence comparison.]  

Reported district-cooling savings of up to 30% require a clearly defined comparison baseline. Similarly, projected solar generation exceeding 3,000 megawatt-hours annually must be considered against total district electricity demand.  

There is also a distinction between relative and absolute efficiency.  

Singapore's electricity generation remains predominantly dependent on natural gas. Therefore, improved efficiency reduces energy demand without eliminating associated emissions.  

Systems thinking also reveals infrastructure dependencies. Centralised cooling offers economies of scale, but its performance depends on occupancy, maintenance and coordinated management, particularly during phased development.  

Finally, ecological sustainability extends beyond energy.  

Pneumatic waste collection may reduce collection traffic without preventing waste generation. Biodiversity walkways may support ecological connectivity, but their effectiveness requires ecological assessment.  

Our conclusion is therefore not that PDD's initiatives are ineffective. Rather, technological efficiency and design certification do not independently establish net ecological sustainability.  

This leads to our next question: do smart technologies also deliver meaningful benefits for people? 

View 5 — Smart City 1.0 versus Smart City 2.0 (1:00)  

Our second perspective examines PDD through the Smart City 2.0 debate.  

Early smart-city models have been criticised for prioritising technological innovation, corporate interests and data-driven optimisation.  

Smart City 2.0 shifts attention towards addressing social challenges and improving human well-being.  

[Point to the Smart City 1.0 versus 2.0 comparison.]  

PDD provides an interesting case because its Open Digital Platform integrates information across district operations through real-time data and digital-twin capabilities.  

This offers opportunities for coordinated management and technological experimentation.  

However, the critical question is not how advanced the technology is, but whether it delivers broader social value beyond operational efficiency.  

View 6 — Smart Operations: Efficiency, Workers and Digital Footprints (1:45)  

One practical example comes from our SIT field visit.  

We learned about sensor-enabled cleaning systems that use information about movement and environmental conditions to support operational decisions.  

[Point to the process diagram.]  

The process involves collecting sensor information, integrating it through building-management systems and using it to inform cleaning operations.  

This may improve productivity by allocating resources according to actual needs.  

However, productivity and human well-being are not necessarily the same.  

For example, does improved monitoring reduce unnecessary workloads and improve working conditions? Or could automation introduce new pressures and skills requirements?  

PDD's automated management ambitions also raise questions about employment transitions.  

Although digital development may create new jobs, these opportunities may require skills different from those affected by automation.  

There is another issue often overlooked in smart-city discussions: digital technologies have their own environmental footprint.  

Sensors, computing infrastructure and hardware require electricity, materials and eventual replacement. Lange and Santarius also highlight how rebound effects can offset some efficiency gains.  

This does not mean digitalisation is environmentally harmful overall. Rather, its net benefits require assessment.  

The wider question is whether technological productivity creates measurable environmental and social value, rather than simply improving existing operations.

Our field visit also highlighted how PDD incorporates human-centred design.

Passive cooling, indoor air-quality monitoring and hybrid ventilation were introduced as approaches to improving occupant comfort while managing energy demand.  

These features demonstrate that PDD's ambitions extend beyond digital infrastructure.  

However, design intentions do not automatically establish actual outcomes.  

Bicycle parking does not necessarily demonstrate increased cycling, while environmental monitoring alone does not establish better health or thermal comfort.  

There is also a question of who benefits.  

PDD's key users include tenants, workers and students. Yet its decentralisation strategy also aims to bring opportunities closer to surrounding residential communities.  

Whether these wider benefits materialise depends on accessibility, employment opportunities and how people use the district.  

This reflects the Smart City 2.0 debate: technological innovation should be evaluated through its contribution to people's needs, not merely its operational performance.  

PDD therefore demonstrates human-centred design ambitions, but evidence of inclusive opportunities and improved everyday experiences remains important.  

This leads to the governance question: who determines which benefits are prioritised and how success is measured? 

View 8 — Governance: Coordination versus Accountability (1:15)  

Our third perspective examines governance, power and justice.  

PDD's governance is state-led and integrated. JTC acts as master planner, developer and landlord, while JTC and GovTech jointly developed the Open Digital Platform.  

This creates an important advantage: coordinated planning and delivery.  

However, institutional coordination also raises questions about who defines development priorities, controls data access and determines acceptable trade-offs.  

Cardullo and Kitchin caution that smart-city participation can position citizens as data sources or service consumers rather than decision-makers.  

Available PDD documentation emphasises institutional collaboration more clearly than mechanisms for users and surrounding communities to influence digital governance.  

This does not establish that participation is absent. Nevertheless, it limits what can currently be demonstrated about participatory accountability.  

Therefore, state-led coordination may strengthen institutional capacity without automatically ensuring public accountability. 

View 9 — Justice: Distribution, Recognition, Capabilities and Participation (1:30)  

Schlosberg's environmental justice framework helps us examine four dimensions.  

[Point to the four-part justice diagram.]  

First, distribution concerns who receives the benefits.  

PDD's planned employment and amenities may benefit surrounding communities, but their accessibility depends on institutional decisions and individual circumstances.  

Second, recognition concerns whose needs are acknowledged.  

The ODP's published descriptions emphasise operational indicators such as energy, cooling and security. However, well-being and everyday experiences require additional forms of evidence.  

Third, capabilities concern whether people gain meaningful opportunities.  

Bringing jobs and education closer to homes may improve access, but residents also need the skills and opportunities to benefit.  

Finally, participation concerns who influences decisions.  

Meadows' leverage-points framework highlights how system goals and rules shape outcomes.  

Applied to PDD, the critical issue is who has the authority to shape these priorities rather than merely operate within predetermined objectives.  

Governance therefore influences what counts as evidence of sustainability. 

View 10 — Integrated SETS Evaluation: What Have We Learned? (1:30) 

Let us now bring the three perspectives together. 

[Return to the integrated SETS diagram.] 

Our ecological analysis showed that efficiency targets and infrastructure capacity do not independently establish net environmental benefits. 

Our Smart City 2.0 analysis showed that operational productivity and human-centred design do not automatically establish inclusive social outcomes. 

Our governance analysis then raised questions about who determines the objectives and indicators used to evaluate these outcomes. 

These concerns are interconnected. 

For example, a sensor-enabled management system may improve operational efficiency, but its wider sustainability contribution also depends on resource consumption, workers' experiences and institutional decisions governing its use. 

This is where the SETS framework becomes particularly useful. 

It encourages us to evaluate technological infrastructure alongside ecological conditions and social institutions, rather than treating them as separate systems. 

Our original interpretation is that PDD's technological performance is more explicitly quantified than its wider ecological and social outcomes in the available project documentation. 

However, an important counterargument must be recognised. 

PDD remains under development, and its state-led coordination offers genuine institutional advantages. Missing operational evidence may reflect timing rather than poor performance. 

Our assessment therefore concerns what can currently be demonstrated, not a definitive judgement that PDD is unsustainable. 

 

View 11 — Recommendations and Conclusion (1:15) 

Based on our evaluation, we propose four priorities. 

First, environmental: publish operational baselines, lifecycle assessments and ecological indicators. 

Second, technological: assess digital infrastructure's resource footprint against its efficiency gains. 

Third, social: evaluate well-being, accessibility and workforce opportunities, including benefits for surrounding communities. 

Fourth, governance: strengthen transparency, independent review and meaningful participation. 

These recommendations are connected. 

Environmental and social indicators provide evidence for evaluation, while participation helps determine which outcomes should receive priority. 

To conclude, PDD demonstrates substantial integration of smart and ecological infrastructure. 

However, its broader sustainability contribution remains promising but conditional. 

For other smart-eco cities, the key lesson is that sustainability must be demonstrated, not assumed.
